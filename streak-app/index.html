🔥 Streak

Habit science, in one place

This page combines book advice and published research on habits, chaining, why habits fail, procrastination and mindfulness. Research means a published study. Book advice comes from practitioners and is usually a good idea but less tested. Full references are at the bottom.

How habits form
  • Cue, routine, reward. A habit runs on a cue that triggers a routine that ends in a reward. To change one, keep the cue and the reward and swap the routine. The Power of Habit, Duhigg (2012)
  • Context and repetition. Habits are tied to the place and situation where you repeat them. In a student diary study, about 43% of behaviors were habitual. Changing your surroundings and adding or removing friction often works better than relying on willpower. Wood, Quinn & Kashy (2002); Good Habits, Bad Habits, Wood (2019)
  • Motivation, ability, prompt. A behavior happens when all three come together. If a habit isn't happening, make it easier or add a clearer prompt. Start tiny, anchor it to something you already do, and celebrate right away. Tiny Habits, Fogg (2019)
  • The four laws. Make it obvious, attractive, easy and satisfying. Add the two-minute rule (shrink it), identity ("I am someone who..."), environment design, never missing twice, and the Goldilocks rule (a task just hard enough). Atomic Habits, Clear (2018)
  • How long it takes. In a study of 96 volunteers, a daily habit took a median of 66 days to feel automatic, ranging from 18 to 254 days. Missing a single day did not materially affect the process. The popular "21 days" figure is not supported by this research. Lally et al. (2010)
Chaining habits
  • Habit stacking. "After I [current habit], I will [new habit]." Each habit becomes the cue for the next. Atomic Habits (Clear); anchoring in Tiny Habits (Fogg)
  • If-then plans are the strongest evidence. Pooling 94 tests, plans of the form "If situation X, then I will do Y" had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (d = 0.65). Gollwitzer (1999); Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006)
  • Showing progress helps. Customers given a loyalty card with 2 of 10 stamps already filled finished it more often (34%) than customers with an empty 8-stamp card (19%), though both needed 8 purchases. Nunes & Drèze (2006)
  • Chains are fragile. One failure can take down every step after it. After a slip, people often think "I've blown it, so why bother", sometimes called the what-the-hell effect or abstinence violation effect. Plan a smaller fallback version of each step and restart at the step that failed. Polivy & Herman (1985); Marlatt & Gordon (1985)
  • "Don't break the chain" (the Seinfeld story) is a popular anecdote, not a research finding.
  • Behavior chaining also appears in applied behavior analysis, where a complex task is taught as a series of linked steps. This is similar to your sub-habits.
When a habit doesn't happen

Treat a miss as a design problem to debug, not a personal failing. These frameworks work as checklists.

  • Fogg model: Was there no prompt, was it too hard, or was motivation low?
  • Atomic Habits laws: Was it not obvious, not attractive, not easy, or not satisfying?
  • COM-B: Do you have the capability, the opportunity and the motivation? Michie, van Stralen & West (2011)
  • WOOP: Name your Wish, the Outcome, the main Obstacle, then make an if-then Plan for it. Imagining only success can lower effort. Oettingen (2014)
  • Pre-mortem: Imagine the habit failed, list why, and fix those causes first. Gary Klein (2007)
  • Track it. A review of 138 experiments (about 20,000 people) found that monitoring progress helps people reach goals, and helps more when it is written down or shared. Harkin et al. (2016)
  • Common reasons: forgot, no time, too tired, too hard or too big, didn't feel like it, travel or a changed routine, sick, something more urgent, or the previous step in a chain failed.
Procrastination
  • Why we do it. Temporal motivation theory: Motivation = (Expectancy × Value) / (Impulsiveness × Delay). A meta-analysis of 691 correlations found the strongest predictors were task unpleasantness, delay of the reward, low self-belief and impulsiveness. Steel (2007)
  • It is about feelings. Procrastination is largely a way to avoid an unpleasant feeling in the short term, so it responds better to emotional strategies than to time-management tricks. Sirois & Pychyl (2013)
  • Forgive yourself. Students who forgave themselves for procrastinating before a first exam procrastinated less before the next one. Wohl, Pychyl & Bennett (2010)
  • Present bias. Rewards that are far away feel smaller than near ones, which favors short-term comfort. Ainslie; Laibson
  • Commitment devices. Self-imposed deadlines help, though deadlines set by someone else work better. Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002)
  • Temptation bundling. Gym-goers who could only hear a gripping audiobook at the gym visited 51% more often in the early weeks. Milkman, Minson & Volpp (2014)
  • Fresh starts. Gym visits, diet searches and goal commitments rise after a new week, month, year or birthday. Dai, Milkman & Riis (2014)
  • Practical tricks such as "just start for 5 minutes", the two-minute rule and Pomodoro sessions are popular, but have thinner research behind them.
Mindfulness
  • What it is. Paying attention to the present moment without judging it. The best-known program is Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction. Kabat-Zinn (1990)
  • Wandering minds. In a study of 2,250 volunteers, minds wandered about 47% of the time, and wandering went with lower happiness. Killingsworth & Gilbert (2010)
  • Urge surfing. Treat a craving as a sensation to be curious about and watch rise and fall. In one smoking trial, mindfulness training had 31% abstinence versus 6% for a standard program at a 4-month follow-up. Brewer, The Craving Mind (2017); Brewer et al. (2011)
  • RAIN. Recognize, Allow, Investigate with kindness, Nurture. Michele McDonald; Brach (2019)
  • Self-compassion. Self-kindness, common humanity and mindful awareness help you recover from a lapse instead of spiraling. Neff (2011)
  • Values-based action. Accept uncomfortable thoughts and feelings and act on what matters to you. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, Hayes et al. (1999)
  • Caution. Much mindfulness research is small or lacks good controls, so treat big claims with care. Van Dam et al. (2018)
How strong is the evidence?
  • Lally's 66 days is a median from 96 people doing simple behaviors. It is not a rule for every habit.
  • Popular claims such as "21 days" and the Seinfeld chain come from books and anecdotes, not studies.
  • Clear and Fogg are practitioners. Their methods are plausible and widely used, but the full systems have not been rigorously tested as a whole.
  • Streaks can backfire if one miss makes you quit, so plan for restarts and keep a minimum version of each habit.
